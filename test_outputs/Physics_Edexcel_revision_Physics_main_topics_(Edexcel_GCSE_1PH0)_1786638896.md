# 1. Key concepts of physics

## 1.1 SI units
You should know the standard SI units for physical quantities, such as:
- length → metre, **m**
- mass → kilogram, **kg**
- time → second, **s**
- current → ampere, **A**
- temperature → kelvin, **K**
- force → newton, **N**
- energy → joule, **J**
- power → watt, **W**
- pressure → pascal, **Pa**
- charge → coulomb, **C**
- potential difference → volt, **V**
- resistance → ohm, **Ω**

## 1.2 Multiples and sub-multiples
Know prefixes:
- giga (G) = 10⁹
- mega (M) = 10⁶
- kilo (k) = 10³
- centi (c) = 10⁻²
- milli (m) = 10⁻³
- micro (μ) = 10⁻⁶
- nano (n) = 10⁻⁹

## 1.3 Unit conversions
Common conversions:
- 1 hour = 3600 s
- 1 km = 1000 m
- 1 cm = 0.01 m
- 1 mm = 0.001 m

## 1.4 Significant figures and standard form
- Use significant figures to match the precision of data.
- Standard form: \(a \times 10^n\), where \(1 \le a < 10\)

# 2. Motion and forces

## Scalars and vectors
- **Scalar**: magnitude only
- **Vector**: magnitude and direction

Examples:
- scalar: speed, distance, mass, energy
- vector: velocity, displacement, force, acceleration, momentum, weight

## Speed and velocity
- **Speed** = distance ÷ time  
  \[
  v = \frac{d}{t}
  \]
- **Velocity** = speed in a stated direction

### Distance-time graphs
- gradient = speed
- steeper line = faster speed
- horizontal line = stationary

## Acceleration
\[
a = \frac{v-u}{t}
\]
where:
- \(a\) = acceleration
- \(v\) = final velocity
- \(u\) = initial velocity
- \(t\) = time

Higher tier equation:
\[
v^2 - u^2 = 2as
\]

### Velocity-time graphs
- gradient = acceleration
- area under graph = distance travelled

## Typical speeds
Know rough values for:
- walking
- running
- cycling
- cars
- sound
- wind

## Free fall and gravity
- acceleration due to gravity: **10 m/s²**
- weight depends on gravitational field strength:
\[
W = mg
\]

## Newton’s laws
### First law
If resultant force is zero:
- object stays at rest, or
- continues at constant velocity

### Second law
\[
F = ma
\]

### Third law
For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

## Momentum
\[
p = mv
\]
Momentum is conserved in collisions in a closed system.

### Force and momentum
\[
F = \frac{\Delta p}{t}
\]

## Stopping distance
Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance

Affected by:
- speed
- reaction time
- mass of vehicle
- brake condition
- road condition
- friction

Reaction time can be affected by:
- tiredness
- alcohol
- drugs
- distractions

## Circular motion
- Velocity changes because direction changes
- Centripetal force acts towards the centre of the circle

# 3. Conservation of energy

## Energy stores and transfers
Energy can be stored in:
- kinetic
- thermal
- chemical
- gravitational potential
- elastic potential
- nuclear
- magnetic
- electrostatic

## Gravitational potential energy
\[
\Delta E_{GPE} = mgh
\]

## Kinetic energy
\[
E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2
\]

## Conservation of energy
Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred.

In a **closed system**:
- total energy stays the same

## Dissipation
Energy is often “wasted” by being transferred to the surroundings, usually as thermal energy.

### Ways to reduce unwanted transfer
- lubrication
- insulation
- reducing friction
- thermal insulation

## Efficiency
\[
\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful energy output}}{\text{total energy input}}
\]

Can be written as a percentage.

## Energy resources
Know advantages and disadvantages of:
- fossil fuels
- nuclear
- biofuels
- wind
- hydroelectric
- tidal
- solar

### Renewable vs non-renewable
- renewable: can be replaced naturally
- non-renewable: finite, will run out

# 4. Waves

## Wave basics
Waves transfer:
- energy
- information

without transferring matter overall.

## Key terms
- **frequency** = number of waves per second (Hz)
- **wavelength** = distance between corresponding points on adjacent waves
- **amplitude** = maximum displacement
- **period** = time for one complete wave
- **wave speed** = how fast a wave travels

## Wave equation
\[
v = f\lambda
\]
and
\[
v = \frac{x}{t}
\]

## Types of wave
### Transverse
- vibrations are perpendicular to wave direction
- examples: electromagnetic waves, seismic S-waves

### Longitudinal
- vibrations are parallel to wave direction
- example: sound

## Refraction, reflection, transmission, absorption
- **reflection**: wave bounces off surface
- **refraction**: wave changes direction because speed changes
- **transmission**: wave passes through
- **absorption**: wave energy transferred to material

## Sound waves
- sound travels as a longitudinal wave
- sound requires a medium
- cannot travel in a vacuum

### Ultrasound
- above 20 000 Hz

### Infrasound
- below 20 Hz

Uses:
- sonar
- foetal scanning
- Earth exploration

## Core practical 4.17
Investigate speed, frequency and wavelength of waves in solids and fluids.

# 5. Light and the electromagnetic spectrum

## Reflection
Law of reflection:
- angle of incidence = angle of reflection

### Specular vs diffuse reflection
- specular: smooth surface, clear reflection
- diffuse: rough surface, scattered reflection

## Refraction
Light changes speed when entering a different medium.
- into denser medium: slows down, bends towards normal
- into less dense medium: speeds up, bends away from normal

## Total internal reflection
Occurs when:
1. light travels from a denser to less dense medium
2. angle of incidence > critical angle

Used in:
- optical fibres
- endoscopes
- some communication systems

## Lenses
### Converging lens
- brings rays together to a focal point
- can produce real or virtual images

### Diverging lens
- spreads rays out
- produces virtual images

## Electromagnetic spectrum
Order from low frequency to high frequency:
1. radio
2. microwaves
3. infrared
4. visible
5. ultraviolet
6. X-rays
7. gamma rays

As frequency increases:
- wavelength decreases
- energy increases
- danger increases

## Uses and dangers
### Radio waves
- broadcasting, communications

### Microwaves
- cooking, satellite communication

### Infrared
- heaters, thermal imaging, remote controls

### Visible light
- vision, photography, lighting

### Ultraviolet
- security marking, fluorescent lamps, sterilising

### X-rays
- medical imaging, airport security

### Gamma rays
- sterilising food/equipment, cancer treatment

## Harmful effects
- microwaves: internal heating
- infrared: skin burns
- ultraviolet: skin cancer, eye damage
- X-rays/gamma: cell mutation, tissue damage

## All electromagnetic waves
- transverse
- travel at the same speed in a vacuum
- transfer energy from source to observer

## Radiation and temperature
All bodies emit infrared radiation:
- hotter objects emit more radiation
- emitted wavelength decreases as temperature increases

### Earth’s temperature
Depends on balance between incoming solar radiation and outgoing infrared radiation.

## Core practical 5.9
Investigate refraction in rectangular glass blocks.

## Core practical 5.19P
Investigate how surface affects thermal radiation absorbed/emitted.

# 6. Radioactivity

## Atom structure
- nucleus: protons and neutrons
- electrons around nucleus
- most mass is in nucleus
- nucleus is very small compared with atom

## Isotopes
Isotopes are atoms of the same element with:
- same number of protons
- different number of neutrons

### Atomic notation
\[
^{13}_{6}\text{C}
\]
- atomic number = number of protons
- mass number = protons + neutrons

## Relative charges and masses
Know:
- proton: charge +1, mass 1
- neutron: charge 0, mass 1
- electron: charge -1, mass very small
- positron: charge +1, mass same as electron

## Ion formation
Atoms become positive ions by losing electrons.

## Nuclear radiation
Unstable nuclei emit:
- alpha
- beta minus
- beta plus
- gamma
- neutron radiation

These emissions are random.

## Ionising radiation
Alpha, beta and gamma are ionising.

## Background radiation
Comes from:
- rocks/soil
- cosmic rays
- radon gas
- medical sources
- food and buildings

## Detection
- photographic film
- Geiger-Müller tube

## Alpha, beta, gamma
### Alpha
- helium nucleus
- strongly ionising
- low penetration

### Beta
- electron from nucleus
- moderate ionisation and penetration

### Gamma
- electromagnetic radiation
- weakly ionising
- high penetration

## Half-life
Half-life = time taken for:
- half the nuclei to decay, or
- activity to halve

Activity unit = **Becquerel (Bq)**

## Calculating decay
Use half-life to determine remaining activity or nuclei after several half-lives.

## Nuclear equations
You must be able to balance by:
- mass number
- charge number

## Uses of radioactivity
- smoke alarms
- food irradiation
- sterilisation
- tracers
- thickness monitoring
- cancer treatment
- PET scanning

## Dangers and precautions
Radiation can damage cells and cause mutations.

Precautions:
- minimise exposure time
- maximise distance
- use shielding
- control dose

## Contamination vs irradiation
- **irradiation**: exposed to radiation from a source
- **contamination**: radioactive material gets onto/in body or environment

## Nuclear fission and fusion
### Fission
- heavy nucleus splits
- releases energy and neutrons
- can cause chain reaction

### Fusion
- light nuclei combine
- releases energy
- powers the Sun and stars

### Nuclear power
Advantages:
- low CO₂ emissions during operation
- high energy output

Disadvantages:
- radioactive waste
- accident risk
- public concern
- expensive to build/decommission

# 7. Astronomy

## Gravity and weight in space
Weight depends on gravitational field strength, so it changes from planet to planet and on the Moon.

## Solar System
Consists of:
- Sun
- 8 planets
- moons
- dwarf planets
- asteroids
- comets

Planets in order:
1. Mercury
2. Venus
3. Earth
4. Mars
5. Jupiter
6. Saturn
7. Uranus
8. Neptune

## Orbits
Objects orbit due to gravity.

For circular orbits:
- speed may stay constant
- velocity changes because direction changes

## Big Bang vs Steady State
### Big Bang
- Universe began from a hot, dense state
- Universe is expanding

### Steady State
- Universe always existed and continues to create matter

## Evidence for Big Bang
- red-shift of galaxies
- cosmic microwave background radiation (CMB)

## Red-shift
Light from distant galaxies shifts to longer wavelength because galaxies are moving away.

Greater red-shift means:
- further away
- faster recession
- supports expanding Universe

## Stars
### Star life cycle for Sun-like stars
nebula → main sequence star → red giant → white dwarf

### Larger stars
nebula → main sequence star → red supergiant → supernova → neutron star or black hole

## Telescopes
Some are placed outside Earth’s atmosphere because the atmosphere:
- absorbs some radiation
- distorts images
- blocks some wavelengths

# 9. Forces and their effects

## Forces
Forces can act:
- at a distance: gravitational, electrostatic, magnetic
- by contact: friction, normal contact force

## Resultant force
The overall force on an object.

- resultant force = 0: balanced forces, constant velocity or rest
- resultant force ≠ 0: acceleration or changing direction

## Free-body diagrams
Show all forces acting on an object.

## Moments
\[
\text{moment} = \text{force} \times \text{distance from pivot}
\]

Principle of moments:
- clockwise moments = anticlockwise moments
- for equilibrium

## Levers and gears
Used to:
- increase force
- change direction of force
- transmit rotational effects

## Lubrication
Reduces friction and unwanted energy transfer.

# 10. Electricity and circuits

## Atomic model of current
Current in metals is a flow of electrons.

## Circuit symbols
Know how to draw:
- cell, battery, switch
- ammeter, voltmeter
- resistor, variable resistor
- lamp
- motor
- diode
- thermistor
- LDR
- LED

## Series and parallel circuits
### Series
- same current through all components
- voltage shared
- resistances add

### Parallel
- same potential difference across branches
- current splits
- total resistance decreases

## Potential difference
\[
V = \frac{E}{Q}
\]
1 volt = 1 joule per coulomb

\[
E = QV
\]

## Current and charge
\[
Q = It
\]

## Resistance
\[
V = IR
\]

## Current at a junction
Current entering a junction = current leaving junction

## I-V characteristics
### Fixed resistor
- straight line through origin

### Filament lamp
- curve because resistance increases as temperature rises

### Diode
- current only in one direction

## LDR
- resistance decreases as light intensity increases

## Thermistor
- resistance decreases as temperature increases
- NTC thermistor only

## Heating effect of current
When current flows through a resistor, energy is transferred to thermal energy.

Caused by collisions between electrons and ions in the lattice.

## Electrical power
\[
P = IV
\]
\[
P = I^2R
\]

## Domestic electricity
UK mains supply:
- a.c.
- 50 Hz
- about 230 V

### Mains wires
- live: dangerous, alternating high potential difference
- neutral: around 0 V
- earth: safety wire, prevents electric shock

### Safety
- fuse and switch should be in live wire
- earth wire and fuse protect against faults

# 11. Static electricity

## Charging by friction
Electrons are transferred between insulating materials.

- gain electrons → negative
- lose electrons → positive

## Charges
- like charges repel
- unlike charges attract

## Electrostatic phenomena
- sparks from clothing
- lightning
- charged balloon attracting a wall
- charged comb picking up paper

## Earthing
Removes excess charge by allowing electrons to flow to or from Earth.

## Uses
- spray painting / insecticide sprayers

## Dangers
- sparking during fuel transfer
- explosions
- earthing prevents dangerous build-up

## Electric fields
An electric field is a region where a charge experiences a force.

Field lines:
- point away from positive
- point towards negative
- closer lines = stronger field

# 12. Magnetism and the motor effect

## Magnetic poles
- like poles repel
- unlike poles attract

## Magnetic materials
- iron, steel, cobalt, nickel

## Permanent vs induced magnets
- permanent magnet: always magnetic
- induced magnet: becomes magnetic in a field

## Magnetic fields
Field lines go from north to south outside the magnet.

### Field strength
Greater where field lines are closer together.

## Earth’s magnetic field
Compass needles align with Earth’s magnetic field, showing Earth behaves like a magnet.

## Magnetic effect of current
A current in a wire produces a magnetic field around it.

## Solenoid
A coil of wire that produces a strong magnetic field when current flows.

Inside a solenoid:
- field is strong and uniform

## Motor effect
A conductor carrying current in a magnetic field experiences a force.

### Fleming’s left-hand rule
Gives direction of:
- force
- field
- current

## Force on a conductor
\[
F = BIl
\]

Used in electric motors to cause rotation.

# 13. Electromagnetic induction

## Induced current
A current is induced when there is relative movement between a magnet and a conductor.

## Factors affecting induced p.d.
- speed of movement
- strength of magnetic field
- number of turns on coil

## Lenz’s law
The induced current opposes the change that produced it.

## Alternators and dynamos
- **alternator**: produces a.c.
- **dynamo**: produces d.c.

## Transformers
A changing current in the primary coil induces a current in the secondary coil.

### Transformer equation
\[
\frac{V_p}{V_s} = \frac{N_p}{N_s}
\]

### Power in an ideal transformer
\[
V_p I_p = V_s I_s
\]

## National Grid
Electricity is transmitted at high voltage to reduce current and reduce energy loss by heating.

Then voltage is stepped down for domestic use.

# 14. Particle model

## States of matter
### Solid
- particles closely packed
- vibrate in fixed positions

### Liquid
- particles close together but can move past each other

### Gas
- particles far apart
- move rapidly and randomly

## Density
\[
\rho = \frac{m}{V}
\]

## Changes of state
Melting, freezing, boiling, evaporation, condensation, sublimation.

- mass is conserved
- physical change, not chemical change

## Internal energy
Energy stored in particles and their motion.

Heating increases:
- temperature, or
- causes change of state

## Specific heat capacity
Energy needed to raise temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1°C.

\[
Q = mc\Delta \theta
\]

## Specific latent heat
Energy needed to change state of 1 kg without temperature change.

\[
Q = mL
\]

## Gas pressure
Caused by collisions of gas particles with container walls.

### Temperature and pressure
If temperature increases, particles move faster, causing more frequent and more forceful collisions.

## Absolute zero
-273 °C
At this point particle motion is at a minimum.

## Pressure in gases
If volume decreases, pressure increases because collisions with walls become more frequent.

For fixed mass and constant temperature:
\[
P_1V_1 = P_2V_2
\]

# 15. Forces and matter

## Deformation
Stretching, compressing or bending requires force.

- **elastic deformation**: object returns to original shape
- **inelastic deformation**: permanent change

## Hooke’s law
\[
F = kx
\]
where:
- \(k\) = spring constant
- \(x\) = extension

## Elastic potential energy in a spring
\[
E = \frac{1}{2}kx^2
\]

## Force-extension graphs
- straight line: linear elastic region
- curve: non-linear, limit of proportionality passed

## Pressure
\[
P = \frac{F}{A}
\]

## Pressure in liquids
\[
P = h\rho g
\]
Pressure increases with:
- depth
- density

## Upthrust
Upthrust is the upward force in a fluid.

- equals the weight of displaced fluid

### Floating and sinking
- object floats if upthrust equals weight
- object sinks if weight is greater than upthrust

# High-Yield Exam Tips

## Always remember:
- write equations clearly
- use correct units
- show working in calculations
- state whether quantities are scalar or vector if asked
- use key terms precisely: energy transfer, resultant force, potential difference, ionising radiation, etc.

## Common mistakes to avoid:
- confusing speed and velocity
- forgetting that current is the same in series
- mixing up mass and weight
- saying energy is “lost” instead of dissipated
- using the wrong units for density, pressure, or charge
- forgetting that gamma is electromagnetic, not a particle
- mixing up refraction and reflection
